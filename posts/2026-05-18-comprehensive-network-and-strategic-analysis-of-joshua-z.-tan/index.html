Comprehensive Network and Strategic Analysis of Joshua Z. Tan

Executive Summary

Joshua Z. Tan represents a highly unusual and deeply influential node within the contemporary intersections of applied mathematics, artificial intelligence policy, and decentralized institutional design. Serving currently as the product and strategy lead for the Public AI movement, an interim Chief Technology Officer at Current AI, and a co-founder and research director at the Metagovernance Project (Metagov), Tan’s work transcends traditional academic boundaries and enters the realm of active institutional engineering [1, 2]. His intellectual trajectory spans from humanistic inquiries into art history and the philosophical concept of the sublime at Yale University to rigorous mathematical modeling of artificial intelligence at the University of Oxford, culminating in a sprawling portfolio of open-source advocacy and digital ethnography [2, 3].

This exhaustive research report provides a multi-dimensional, deeply contextualized analysis of Tan’s public profile. It traces his biographical and intellectual evolution, catalogs his notable achievements within academia and public policy, and dissects the philosophical and theoretical underpinnings of his work on subsymbolic organizations, applied category theory, and public-interest technology [1, 2]. Crucially, this document maps out a comprehensive intelligence picture of his professional and personal network, identifying the dense web of co-authors, co-founders, collaborators, and friends who interact with him across various digital and academic frontiers. Through this analysis, a clear picture emerges of an institutional entrepreneur who leverages higher mathematics and collective intelligence frameworks to build equitable governance layers for the future of the internet [4, 5].

In compiling this intelligence, it is necessary to establish precise digital disambiguation. The public record contains multiple individuals sharing this name, including a Doctor of Musical Arts graduate from USC who operates as a faculty member at Fullerton College, a cybersecurity researcher who completed a PhD at Carnegie Mellon University in 2020 specializing in authentication systems, a storyteller and film producer, and an architect holding degrees from Cambridge and Yale [6, 7, 8, 9]. The subject of this report, Joshua Z. Tan, is strictly the mathematician, Oxford DPhil candidate, and Harvard Berkman Klein fellow whose work concentrates on AI, topology, and online governance [1, 4, 10].

Epistemological and Biographical Foundations

To fully comprehend Joshua Tan’s current strategic initiatives in artificial intelligence and decentralized governance, one must first analyze his highly transdisciplinary educational background. His academic evolution demonstrates a deliberate progression from the study of human aesthetics and perception to the mathematical architectures that govern machine learning, and finally to the sociology of collective behavior. This trajectory is not a sequence of disconnected interests, but rather a cohesive investigation into how complex systems—whether artistic, mechanical, or social—process information and generate emergent properties.

Tan’s undergraduate tenure at Yale University laid an unconventional foundation for a future mathematician and computer scientist. At Yale, he dual-majored in Ethics, Politics, and Economics (EP&E) alongside Humanities, with a pronounced concentration in Art History [2, 3]. His senior thesis focused on the concept of the “sublime,” a philosophical and aesthetic concept deeply associated with greatness beyond all possibility of measurement or imitation [2, 3]. The historical study of the sublime often involves engaging with the overwhelming scale of natural environments or the limits of human sensory perception, connecting ideas of aesthetic judgment to central institutions of the modern era, including the public sphere and the market [11, 12, 13]. In retrospect, this early fascination with the sublime serves as a critical conceptual precursor to his later investigations into collective intelligence and emergent artificial neural networks. Both of these phenomena operate as highly complex, practically unmeasurable systems that defy simple reductionist logic, requiring entirely new vocabularies to describe their scale and impact on human society.

Simultaneous to his humanistic studies at Yale, Tan engaged in highly technical empirical work in cognitive robotics. He served as a researcher at Yale’s ScazLab (the Social Robotics Lab), directed by Brian Scassellati [3, 14]. Within this environment, Tan programmed robots for human-robot interaction (HRI) experiments, focusing on the mechanics of nonverbal behavior and perception [3, 14]. This work resulted in a heavily cited research paper co-authored with Henny Admoni, Caroline Bank, and Mariya Toneva, titled “Robot gaze does not reflexively cue human attention,” which was published in the Proceedings of the 33rd Annual Conference of the Cognitive Science Society [14, 15]. This dual existence—synthesizing the philosophical abstraction of art history with the hard empirical coding of human-machine interaction—established a pattern of cross-disciplinary synthesis that continues to define his entire career.

The Mathematical Architecture of Intelligence

Following his time at Yale, Tan transitioned into rigorous mathematical theory, undertaking graduate studies that sought to map the foundational architecture of artificial intelligence [4]. During his Master of Science studies, he worked directly under the tutelage of Misha Gromov, a highly decorated geometer and mathematician renowned for his revolutionary work in geometry and topology [2, 3].

Under Gromov’s advisement, Tan authored a master’s thesis exploring the highly speculative connections between cohomology and learning algorithms [2, 3]. Cohomology, a sophisticated concept in algebraic topology, involves assigning algebraic invariants to topological spaces to classify their properties and understand their structural holes. By applying topological concepts to machine learning, Tan sought to establish universal structural laws governing how artificial systems process, retain, and categorize information. This transition marked his formal entry into the world of applied higher mathematics, setting the stage for his subsequent doctoral work. Tan ultimately pursued his DPhil (PhD) and a subsequent postdoctoral placement at the University of Oxford, formally operating as a mathematician and computer scientist where he continued to develop the cohomological theories of learning established during his master’s degree [1, 3, 4].

Beyond his specific doctoral research, Tan became an active ecosystem builder within a specific mathematical movement known as Applied Category Theory (ACT). Category theory is a highly abstract branch of mathematics that deals with structures and systems of structures, emphasizing the relationships (morphisms) between objects rather than the objects themselves. Traditionally viewed as “pure” mathematics, the ACT movement seeks to apply categorical logic to real-world domains such as quantum physics, natural language processing, database architecture, and complex game theory [3, 16, 17].

Tan has been instrumental in formalizing this nascent field. In 2018, he served as a primary organizer for the seminal Applied Category Theory workshop at the Lorentz Center in Leiden, the Netherlands, alongside Bob Coecke, Brendan Fong, Aleks Kissinger, and Martha Lewis [17, 18]. This workshop was explicitly designed to map the current ACT landscape and develop a roadmap for transitioning the field to concrete applications [19]. Stemming from this initiative, Tan served on the founding executive board and as an executive editor for Compositionality, a peer-reviewed, open-access academic journal dedicated to compositional ideas in science and mathematics stemming from categorical origins [2, 3, 4, 16]. Furthermore, he continues to co-edit the Applied Category Theory book series published by Cambridge University Press alongside Oxford’s Bob Coecke [3, 4]. Tan’s entrepreneurial ventures also intersect with this mathematical domain; he served as the entrepreneurial lead for Categorical Informatics, a math and data company spun out of the MIT mathematics department [2].

Institutional Engineering and Subsymbolic Organizations

Tan’s mathematical insights into complex systems eventually found a highly practical application in the realm of internet governance and digital sociology. He is a co-founder and research director at the Metagovernance Project (Metagov), a laboratory dedicated to the engineering of digital institutions [1, 2, 4]. Operating at the vanguard of Web3, decentralized autonomous organizations (DAOs), and digital sociology, Metagov functions as a transdisciplinary think-tank building computational tools for online governance and facilitating digital ethnography [20, 21].

Within Metagov, Tan leads research on “subsymbolic organizations”—entities governed by implicit, cultural, or non-formalized rules that defy strict algorithmic coding [2]. The challenge of subsymbolic organizations is that human coordination cannot always be reduced to smart contracts or binary code; cultural polysemy and unwritten social contracts play a massive role in how communities govern themselves [21]. To address this, Tan leads the “To Community” project, an initiative funded by a consortium of major philanthropic entities including the National Science Foundation (NSF), the Ford Foundation, the Alfred P. Sloan Foundation, the Omidyar Network, and Open Collective [2]. This project builds calculators to model governance transitions within open-source communities and guides organizations through Exit-to-Community (E2C) frameworks [2].

Tan’s academic output in this domain frequently addresses the “building blocks of Web 3.0” and the necessity of constitutional frameworks for digital spaces [3]. His theoretical approach suggests that all intelligence—whether human, machine, or organizational—is fundamentally collective in nature [1, 15]. In 2021, he co-authored “Modular Politics: Toward a Governance Layer for Online Communities” alongside Nathan Schneider, Primavera De Filippi, Seth Frey, and Amy X. Zhang [15, 22]. This heavily cited paper outlines a paradigm shift away from monolithic platform governance toward modular, composable governance tools, directly translating the mathematical concept of compositionality into political science.

He is also a central figure in the academic formalization of DAOs. He has authored massive collaborative survey papers such as “Open Problems in DAOs” and participated in extensive digital ethnographies of blockchain governance alongside researchers like Ellie Rennie and Michael Zargham [2, 15, 21]. To advance this field, Tan has actively collaborated to build the Validator Commons and organized major academic summits like the “DAO Harvard” conference in 2023 and the DAO Asia Summit in Bangkok in 2024 to formalize the intersection of cryptoeconomics and collective governance [2, 23, 24]. Furthermore, he expanded his influence through pedagogical roles, co-teaching a course titled “Governing Many Worlds” alongside Zargham at Carnegie Mellon University in the spring of 2023 [1, 2].

The Geopolitics of Artificial Intelligence and Public Infrastructure

In recent years, Tan has positioned himself as a leading architect, advocate, and strategist for the “Public AI” movement. This initiative advocates for artificial intelligence systems that are publicly accountable, broadly accessible, and strictly resistant to private corporate enclosure [1, 5]. He currently leads product and strategy for Public AI (capitalized), operating foundational infrastructure such as the Public AI Inference Utility alongside Joseph Low and the Public AI Libraries Project alongside Mohsin Yousufi [2, 25].

The genesis of this coordinated movement can be traced to the summer of 2023 at the Internet Archive’s DWeb Camp, an unconventional conference for the decentralized web held in the redwood forests of California. It was here that Tan, alongside Nick Garcia from Public Knowledge and SJ Klein from the Concordance Project, established the strategic foundation for the Public AI Network [5]. Supported by entities like Aspen Digital and the Rockefeller Foundation, the network grew from a small group of academics into an international coalition boasting over 100 organizations [5]. Tan subsequently organized an “unconference” called AI Palace in July 2023 to further consolidate this community [2].

Tan’s advocacy is deeply rooted in economic theory. He argues that frontier AI models exhibit the fundamental characteristics of public goods: they are non-rivalrous, widely reusable, and generate massive productivity spillovers across the entire economy [26]. If left strictly to free-market dynamics, Tan and his collaborators argue that these technologies will be undersupplied to the public and overly enclosed by private corporate monopolies, leading to an optimal social deficit [26].

To counteract this inevitable enclosure, Tan has co-authored highly ambitious, international policy proposals. The most prominent of these is the “Airbus for AI” framework, which proposes a multi-national, publicly backed consortium for AI development, explicitly modeled on the European aerospace consortium that successfully broke an American monopoly in aviation [2, 26]. This proposal received input from leading figures in economics and AI, including Yoshua Bengio, Paul Seabright, and Ricardo Baeza-Yates [26]. Furthermore, alongside Geoff Mulgan, Saffron Huang, Tom Malone, and Lewis Hammond, Tan developed a proposal for an “IPCC for AI”—an international, intergovernmental panel for artificial intelligence monitoring and safety governance modeled after the United Nations climate change institutions [2].

Currently, Tan holds a fellowship at the Berkman Klein Center for Internet & Society at Harvard University. His stated mission at the center is to build specific protocols for collaboration and peer production that enable diverse groups of people across teams, companies, and international borders to build shared AI systems collectively [1]. This work explicitly attempts to synthesize his prior research in Metagov—which focused on how to govern distributed, trustless teams—with the pressing geopolitical challenge of AI democratization. He has also advocated for these concepts within the machine learning community directly, co-authoring a workshop paper and poster on public AI for the RegML track at the prestigious NeurIPS 2023 conference alongside Nick Vincent, Sarah Schwettmann, and David Bau [2].

Alternative Capital and Experimental Finance

A unique facet of Tan’s operational profile is his direct engagement with experimental investment vehicles and venture operations, treating financial capital allocation as an applied extension of his theories on institutional design. Theoretical governance models must eventually interact with actual economic stakes to prove their viability.

To this end, Tan co-runs an experimental investment club named “Filigreen” alongside John Garry, Joseph Low, and James Waugh [2]. The presence of Joseph Low in this venture is particularly notable, as Low also holds an overlapping administrative and operational role for the Public AI Inference Utility [2, 25]. This overlap indicates a significant bleed-over between Tan’s financial experimentation and his public infrastructure initiatives, suggesting that experimental investment models may be utilized to fund or sustain public-interest technology. Tan’s engagement with capital allocation also extends to the Web3 hackathon space; he recently noted starting a “bank” for DAOs at a hackathon, a project which ultimately won the competition [2].

Furthermore, Tan frequently interacts with the venture capital ecosystem that surrounds the blockchain space. He has participated in high-level policy summits, such as the “Perspectives on Web3” event hosted by the Belfer Center, where he shared panels with prominent venture partners from Pantera Capital, a16z Crypto, and G20 Ventures [27]. This positions Tan not as an academic isolated from the market, but as a strategist actively negotiating with the primary allocators of capital in the decentralized web ecosystem.

Scientometrics and Academic Impact

Tan’s scholarly impact is substantial, maintaining a robust presence in academic literature across cognitive science, human-computer interaction, mathematical topology, digital sociology, and institutional design. According to publicly available scientometric tracking, his work has garnered 460 total citations across his career, with a consistent upward trajectory in recent years [15]. As of 2026, he holds an h-index of 10 and an i10-index of 10, demonstrating a solid baseline of highly cited research output [15].

His most heavily cited works reflect the dual nature of his early and current career. His 2021 paper “Modular Politics: Toward a governance layer for online communities” has accumulated 110 citations, cementing it as a foundational text for DAO governance and digital institutional design [15]. Similarly, his 2011 paper from his time at Yale’s ScazLab, “Robot gaze does not reflexively cue human attention,” remains highly influential within cognitive robotics, holding 103 citations [15]. His massive collaborative surveys, such as “Toward a participatory digital ethnography of blockchain governance” (2022) and “Open problems in DAOs” (2023), reflect his capacity to synthesize broad fields of inquiry and serve as central nodes for subsequent research [15].

Thematic and Strategic Implications

A comprehensive synthesis of Joshua Tan’s public profile reveals a highly intentional strategy that bridges the gap between pure mathematics and applied political science. The transition from the philosophical study of the “sublime” to the mathematical study of neural networks, and finally to the political advocacy of Public AI, represents a cohesive attempt to manage systems that exceed human cognitive capacity [2, 26].

Furthermore, his mobilization of the Applied Category Theory movement through the Compositionality journal and Cambridge University Press underscores a belief that human institutions can be engineered with the same composable, modular rigor as software databases [3, 4, 16]. The Metagovernance Project functions as the primary laboratory for this thesis, actively testing how digital communities can build interoperable governance structures [20]. By surrounding himself with top-tier collaborators across law, digital ethnography, computer science, and venture capital, Joshua Tan has successfully constructed the intellectual and operational infrastructure necessary to significantly influence the next generation of internet governance and public-interest artificial intelligence.
